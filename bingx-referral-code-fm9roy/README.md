## Record the invitation without treating it as a performance measure

This distinction is useful when a registration goes smoothly but an account display looks unfamiliar. Resolve the display question on its own terms rather than assuming that every unexpected number must be caused by referral attribution.

## A transfer does not create a purchase price

BingX's [spot profit-and-loss explanation](https://bingx.com/en/support/articles/14624113425807) states that assets moved from the Fund Account to the Spot Account may initially show no cost price or trading PnL because the transfer is not a market purchase. A blank display should not be interpreted as a zero purchase cost or as proof that the assets have disappeared.

If you acquired the asset before transferring it, retain your original acquisition record for your own analysis. The receiving platform may not possess the information needed to reconstruct that earlier history. A manually entered analytical cost, where the interface permits it, is still only as reliable as the records used to create it.

Keep the quantity and the history separate. You can verify how much of an asset is present without yet having a complete explanation of its historical cost. Avoid “fixing” a missing display by inventing a purchase price.

## Distinguish added capital from a gain

Suppose a hypothetical account starts a review period with assets worth 1,000 units. You add another 500 units during the period, and the ending value is 1,470 units. Assuming no other external flows and a consistent valuation method, the 470-unit increase in the displayed balance is not a 470-unit gain. Relative to the 1,500 units of starting value plus added capital, the account is 30 units lower.

This simple example does not attempt to calculate a formal investment return. It shows why a raw beginning-to-ending balance comparison is incomplete when money moves in or out. Timing, prices and the valuation of different assets may require further work for a more detailed analysis.

Label external contributions and withdrawals clearly. Internal transfers between your own account sections should not be counted twice as new money. Doing so can make one screen look profitable while the combined record tells a different story.

## Add the fee effect to the correct transaction

For an individual completed purchase and sale, begin with what was paid and what was received. Then account for the applicable fees in their actual currencies. If you convert them into one reporting currency, keep the original amounts and state the conversion basis.

As a deliberately simplified illustration, assume an asset costs 250 units before a 0.25-unit purchase fee and is later sold for 270 units before a 0.27-unit sale fee. Net sale proceeds would be 269.73 units and total purchase cost would be 250.25 units, leaving 19.48 units before any other relevant costs. These figures are hypothetical and are not BingX fee quotations.

If a confirmed referral arrangement later credits part of the eligible fees, record that credit once. Choose one consistent method: retain the gross fee and record the credit separately, or record the net fee once. Do not use both methods for the same credit. The purpose of the record is to reconcile actual entries, not maximize a displayed saving.

## A short review process

Start by checking quantities. Next, identify external transfers and internal movements. Then review completed trades, the associated fees and any separately recorded credits. Finally, examine how the interface values the remaining holdings. This order reduces the chance that a display issue will be confused with missing funds or a missing referral benefit.

You do not need a complex spreadsheet for a small account. A consistent note showing the transaction type, date, asset, amount and source record is often enough to make an unclear number explainable.
