## Start with three quantities

When planning a small order, distinguish the total asset balance, the balance currently available, and the quantity the market permits you to enter. Those numbers can differ for ordinary reasons.

An open order may reserve part of the balance. A market may require orders to meet a minimum size or use a specific quantity increment. A completed transaction may also leave a small remainder after fees and permitted rounding. None of these observations, by itself, proves that an invitation failed.

MEXC's [spot-order FAQ](https://www.mexc.com/support/article/mexc-spot-order-faq-338746501587991552) discusses balances reserved for pending orders and residual holdings caused by quantity precision. Use the rules shown for the particular pair; do not borrow the minimum from another asset merely because both trade against the same quote currency.

## A small remainder does not identify its cause

Suppose a hypothetical market accepts sell quantities in increments of 0.1 units, and your available balance is 10.07 units. Entering 10.07 might not fit that increment. Selling 10.0 could leave 0.07 units even if the trading fee were zero. This example illustrates a quantity rule, not a current MEXC setting for any specific asset.

Now consider a different case in which the balance is partly reserved by another order. The same “not enough available” message could have a different explanation. Check open orders and the available balance before trying to solve the issue by depositing more.

The useful sequence is to identify the exact rule involved, then decide whether the remaining amount needs any action. Repeatedly submitting a rejected order with slightly different numbers may obscure the problem without improving the outcome.

## Small does not mean immune to execution costs

MEXC's [market-order guide](https://www.mexc.com/support/article/how-to-use-market-orders-in-spot-trading-332255423024245760) explains that market execution can differ from the displayed price, particularly when available liquidity is limited. An order's small monetary size does not remove the need to inspect the relevant market and its conditions.

Before deciding to buy or sell solely to tidy a balance, compare the purpose of the transaction with the likely costs. There may be a bid-ask difference, a trading charge, a minimum size or another constraint. A successful order can remove an inconvenient remainder while still costing more than the remainder was worth to you.

A limit price can constrain an acceptable execution price, but it does not guarantee that the order will fill. Leaving a small order open may also keep funds reserved. Make the decision on the transaction's purpose rather than assuming that every displayed balance needs to be converted immediately.
