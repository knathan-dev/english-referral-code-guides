## Decide what participation is worth before counting rewards

Begin with the reason you want to use the service. Learning its interface, understanding an eligible activity, or evaluating whether it suits an existing workflow can have practical value. A larger points total alone does not tell you whether the experiment was worthwhile.

Write a short purpose statement and a spending limit before connecting those decisions to incentives. For example, someone exploring a service might allow a hypothetical twelve units of total expense for the month. That is an expense ceiling, not an estimate of future token income. A referral benefit should not silently increase it.

## Separate reversible exploration from exposure

Reading instructions and checking an invitation are different commitments from moving assets or carrying out transactions. Move through those decisions separately. Establish which actions you understand before considering actions that expose capital or incur a fee.

The official points documentation explains eligible participation and weekly point allocation. It does not make every action economically sensible for every participant. If an action has a cost you cannot explain, pause at that step. There is no need to create more activity simply to make a referral percentage look useful.

## Calculate the boost in its own unit

Consider a purely hypothetical allocation of 1,450 eligible base points. If an account actually qualifies for the supplied 10% boost, the arithmetic would add 145 points, producing 1,595. Both the original quantity and the extra quantity are points.

There is no exchange rate hidden in that calculation. Multiplying by an imagined future token price would introduce an unsupported assumption. Keep any real expenses in their actual currency and the reward quantity in points. This preserves a clear distinction between something spent today and an uncertain benefit that may have different conditions later.

## Set a stop rule that survives enthusiasm

A stop rule works best when it can be checked without predicting a market. Examples include reaching your expense ceiling, encountering an unfamiliar transaction, or discovering that the activity is outside your original purpose. These are personal decision rules, not Metric requirements.

Suppose an extra activity costs four units and appears likely to increase points. If it pushes the experiment above your twelve-unit allowance, the existence of a boost does not repair the budget. You can decline the activity while still retaining what you learned from the smaller experiment.

## Treat a missing boost as an information problem

First check whether the invitation was associated with the intended wallet and whether the activity falls within the current eligibility rules. Note the relevant period and distinguish a displayed base amount from an expected total. Weekly allocation means that comparing records from different periods can create misleading differences.

If something remains unclear, ask the operator about that specific discrepancy. A useful question names the wallet context, applicable period and rule you are trying to understand. It does not require disclosing a seed phrase or making another transaction to demonstrate commitment. Avoid assuming that repeating an action will fix attribution.

## Review the experiment without a resale forecast

At the end of your chosen period, evaluate three outcomes: whether the service met your original purpose, whether you stayed within the limit, and whether you understood the transactions you approved. Points can be recorded as an additional outcome without becoming the sole measure of success.

That review can support a decision to continue, reduce activity or stop. None requires a prediction about future distributions. The strongest reason to continue is that the underlying activity remains appropriate under terms you understand, even when the promotional component is uncertain.
