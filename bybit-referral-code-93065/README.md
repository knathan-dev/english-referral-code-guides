## What changes when an order meets the order book

The order book contains offers to buy and sell at specified prices. An execution that immediately matches available liquidity is a taker execution. An order that rests on the book and is later matched by someone else supplies liquidity and is treated as maker activity.

Bybit's [maker and taker explanation](https://www.bybit.com/en/help-center/article/Comparison-Between-Maker-Orders-and-Taker-Orders) makes an important distinction: taker activity can come from a market order or from a limit order. A limit order controls the worst price you are willing to accept, but a limit price can still be immediately executable against an existing order.

This matters before you multiply a headline referral percentage by a fee estimate. If you assumed a maker rate but the order took existing liquidity, the starting fee in your calculation may already be wrong. The referral arrangement does not change the historical fact of how the order matched.

## A limit price and a lower fee solve different problems

Consider a hypothetical buyer who sets a limit price above an available selling price. The order may be able to execute immediately within the buyer's limit. Calling the order a “limit order” would be accurate; assuming that every resulting execution must pay a maker fee would not.

Now consider a limit order placed at a price that no seller currently accepts. It may wait on the book, fill later, fill only partly, or remain unfilled. Waiting can preserve price control, but there is no assurance that the planned transaction will happen. A cheaper fee attached to an execution that never occurs is not a realized saving.

Choose an order instruction for its execution purpose. A referral benefit is an additional term to evaluate, not a reason to choose an order that conflicts with the timing or price constraints of a decision you have already made.

## Work through one deliberately hypothetical example

Assume two otherwise comparable planned executions each have a value of 2,000 USDT. For illustration only, use a maker rate of 0.02% and a taker rate of 0.05%. The corresponding fees would be 0.40 USDT and 1.00 USDT.

If an account had a confirmed 20% reduction applying to each entire fee, the reductions would be 0.08 USDT and 0.20 USDT. The remaining fees would be 0.32 USDT and 0.80 USDT. The larger reduction on the second fee would still leave a higher fee. A bigger “amount saved” does not necessarily describe a cheaper transaction.

The example does not quote Bybit's current rates, establish the terms of 93065, or account for differences in execution price. Those missing inputs can matter more than the arithmetic. Compare the final outcome relevant to your plan rather than celebrating the largest discount amount.

## Use the execution record when investigating a charge

When a completed transaction looks different from your estimate, gather the order identifier, execution time, traded value, fee currency and actual liquidity classification where available. An order that fills in several parts may require reviewing its individual execution entries rather than only the order summary.

Check that the percentage in your calculation uses the same units as the recorded fee. A charge in one asset and a trade value in another require a clearly stated conversion basis. Round only at the end of a comparison; rounding many tiny executions first can produce an apparent discrepancy.

For an unresolved account issue, ask official support about that specific execution and the account's referral terms. There is no need to share an API secret, password or verification code with someone who supplied an invitation.
